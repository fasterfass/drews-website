Lately, I've been thinking a lot about what type of life I want to lead. I've been blessed to have a bredth of varied experiences where I've been exposed to many priveledged spaces with a lot of talented people. With that access, I feel I've gained some perspective on what games are worth playing and which are false peaks (at least for myself). Through the past decade I've bought myself some time to take a step back and re-prioritize my life, so I'm hoping to use that perspective and time to be more thoughtful with where I invest myself to build the future I want and decide what type of person I want to become.


### The Power or Youth & Naivete 🏃

For the first 25 years of my life, the theme of my life was learning and grinding. I said yes to the opportunities that came along and did my best to work hard and learn from the people around me to make the most of those experiences. There was some discression and discernment to which path I took, but for the most part I was much more focused on *how* to do a good job and on *being* a person that does a good job, than I was on choosing *what* job to do. I was lucky to learn at a young age that hard work and consistency delivers results, and because I didn't necessarily know *what* I wanted to do, I believed that as long as I fully invested myself into something I could learn to excel at anything. 

This model worked great for me for a long time. This core belief (of hard work + consistency = success) led me to choose the hard route often. For college, I chose the hard route of leaving my home to go out of state to reinvent myself. I chose the hard route of majoring in Electrical Engineering, a path that I wasn't naturally gifted at. I chose the hard route of working at Tesla and putting my head down there to produce value and climb the software engineering career ladder. Even outside of school and work, I leveraged this belief to choose to set hard goals and push for new heights every day. I chose to run my first marathon on graduation day. I chose to bike through cold/rainy weather across the 520 bridge every day to get to work. And even in my free time, I chose to climb mountains (Mt. Rainier & Mt. Adams) - not because it was easy but specifically because it was hard. A major part of my identity was built on this validation cycle. Working hard and achieving difficult goals helped me prove to myself that I was the type of person that didn't shy away from a challenge and that my efforts made me worthy of love/success/etc. It gave me a sense of control and internal validation to know that if I showed up consistently I could get the outcomes I wanted out of life. Externally, it was validated often as well. People we're proud of my achievements and saw my resume and life highlights as a sign that I was on a promising path. My career resonated with a lot of folks and opened a lot of doors for me. Famously, everyone loves the underdog narrative that with hard work and consistency, anyone can tackle pretty much anything. 

But this way of life came with some serious tradeoffs. Pinning my self worth and identity to the need to constantly work harder and deliver outcomes (for both myself and other people) led me into an unsustainable positive feedback loop. I needed to keep the treadmill moving to validate myself. It put me in a lot of positions where I felt like I was always making the "smart" choice (and speeding the treadmill up for the sake of status, security, validation, etc...) instead of the choice that resonated more intrinsically with where my heart lied. I often had expectations for myself that were unrealistic and unfair, putting myself in mental loops where regardless of how I spent my time I felt like it was never enough. Either I would be letting myself down by not striving hard enough for the next thing (in which case I pushed myself to work harder) or I was letting other people down by opting out and not continuing to climb the (very lucrative) ladder they graciously gave me access to. I wanted to please everyone, but did so at the expense of myself. 


### Forced Perspective 🧐

I think some degree of grit to conquer challenges that come is a necessary part of life. I'm very thankful for my past and for the fact that I am willing to work hard to achieve a goal. In aggregate, I think that trait has given me more than it has taken away. But there is always a limit, and sometimes the limit is made obvious to you by forces outside of your control. For me, this happened in 2021 when my dad got brain cancer.

When my dad got cancer, my life flipped upside down. I learned very quickly (with sobering clarity) how I was spending my time. I became very aware of the burden that Tesla was on my life and my identity. Previously, I had seen it as primarily a good opportunity - a logical next step with name recognition that would set me up well and open doors for me in the future. It symbolized a focal point of stability in my life - one that I could build the rest of my life on top of as it offered a stable salary, consistent source of meaning, and the promise of future growth (why leave the rocketship as it's taking off?). But now, my rose colored glasses were not quite so rosy. While it was a good opportunity and one that gave me financial stability, it was a job that came with a lot of tradeoffs. The expectations were high, there was very litte seperation between work and home life, and there was not much flexibility to sort out personal items. You were expected to deliver, and there was not much redundancy available to buffer you if you needed support. Everyone was consistently under pressure to deliver on condensed deadlines, and as a result many people let their personal lives deprioritize to accomidate the role. I was no different.

At the time my dad was going through chemotherapy, I was balancing my new reality while acting as the lead (and sole) engineer on a new product that was slated to go live in November that year (cancer diagnosis happened in September). In July, I had told my team I was leaving and moving internally to the energy software org (unrelated to the cancer diagnosis but happened to be going on at the same time). They told me I couldn't leave until I finished this project because they had no backup plan for who would cover it if I left (no redundancy). I couldn't hand the project off because of tight deadlines. I couldn't recruit more people because other people were equally busy with other high priority projects. Even if people had been available, I didn't have time to train them. On top of this, I was still relied upon on as a core piece of the payments infrastructure team (which supported global payments 24/7/365 with little room for error) and the primary owner of the subscription stack (doing $25+ million in revenue every month in the critical path of multiple customer-facing products). I had too many items on my plate, not enough flexibility to remove or delegate them to other people, and not enough experience and confidence to say no more forcefully. Across the board, it was a 💩 situation that squeezed me to a level I hadn't experienced before.

Eventually, this level of pressure and stress broke me. I delivered what I needed to deliver but got burned out in the process and lost my motivation and faith to continue down the path I was on. I realized I had become too focused on the *how* (how to deliver faster? / how to avoid conflict? / how to balance exceedingly lofty goals?) and not focused enough on the *why* (why do you want this goal in the first place? / why are you investing so much of yourself into a direction you don't care about? / why are you letting other peoples priorities take you further away from things you love in a time of need?). It didn't happen overnight, but over time these experiences led me to the realization that this work grind was unsustainable - that if I didn't change, I'd fall back into the same stressors that had brought me to this point, causing me to lose focus on what truly mattered in my life.

I needed to reframe and change my definition of "success".


### Finding Balance 🙏

Fast forward to today, I've made many changes to regain balance in my life. About a year ago I chose to quit Tesla and to take a step back from work to reorder my priorities. This gave me much more time to focus on understanding myself better and investing myself more in alignment with my intrinsic goals (even if those goals didn't "logically" make sense extrinsically to other people thru the narrow lens of finances/status/expectation/etc). I started going to therapy to begin rewriting the narrative of my life and decoupling my identity and self-worth from the accomplishment treadmill. I had to officially close out the book on my past chapter of life to shift my focus toward the future, and therapy gave me a forum to do that (through providing me a consistent, unbiased lens to understand my psyche and reframe my narratives about work, life, and relationships that no longer served me).

On top of working on my mental health, I also invested more of myself into improving my physical health. I spent more time running around town, playing soccer with friends, and swimming. The focus shifted away from "how do I get this done ✅ amidst other stuff in my life?" to "how do I feel good as often as possible?" and "what do I feel like doing today?". Moving from a mental framework of "have to" to "want to" helped me improve my relationship to exercise and my overall wellbeing. By leaning into what I intrinsically feel like doing, I've been able to improve my fitness and feel better more consistently, with less overhead on my life. It's a balance I'm not going to have forever but one that has made me appreciate that life is a lot easier when you have a solid mental and physical health foundation.

Lastly, I put more focus into slowing down. My Dad's cancer taught me a lot about reframing what's important in life. He spent a lot of his 30's, 40's, and 50's working in jobs he didn't love to build up a stable financial future for our family and to setup himself and my mom for retirement. But when retirement came, and he shortly after got the cancer diagnosis it threw his past decisions of where he put his time into question.



While physical health has always been a big part of my life (through exercise, sport, etc.), my Dad's cancer experience hammered home to me the importance of putting health first above all else. Having good health (both mental and physical) is fundamental to everything we do. It shapes our ability to operate day in and day out. It dictates our lifespan and our healthspan. 


It's the one currency that can't be bought. - you just have to make it a priority and put time into it to get results. At the time I left Tesla, I was physically not in great shape.

Good health is the foundation of everything I want to do. 

Without health, you have nothing I've always been someone that's been fairly physically active, but have never had the time to make that a primary focus.

 This forum also gave me dedicated space to focus on how to improve my relationships with those around me and to introspect on what I truly wanted out of life given this new post-cancer lens. More than anything else, just being consistent in showing up for myself to focus on my mental health did wonders for my psyche.  It allowed me to work through some strongly-held narratives and regain control over my story and my  . It's an ongoing practice but learning how to shift from a mindset of fear into one of curiosity and motivation is the goal.

I started spending more time improving my physical and mental health, making it a priority to focus on feeling better and more at peace instead of feeling rushed to tackle the next task. In a world that seemed to be speeding up, I was putting more effort to slow myself down. I wanted to enjoy more of my days and live in a better headspace more often.
