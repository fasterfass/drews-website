## Unavoidable Fundamental Question(s)

A few weeks ago, I turned 30 (🎉!!) and while I've never really been one to ascribe too much weight to my birthday the experience has given me some room to reflect on my past decade and look forward to the next. With that reflection, one fundamental question keeps coming up. It has many different forms and pops up in many different contexts (relationships, family, work, self, etc), but the core premise is always the same...

> *How do I live my best life?*

This is a huge question and one with way too many broad strokes to tackle in one piece. If you get too close to it or try to define it too explicitly, it generally finds a way to wriggle away from your grasp through forcing a change in perspective (and thus forcing you to re-ask the question). 

In my early 20's I was living my "best" life in terms of my financial future and my career progression. I was able to work my way up the career ladder at a well respected company while saving up enough money to set myself up financially for the long term. Through the narrow lens of my bank account and my LinkedIn profile I was living my "best" life. But this life came at the cost of my free time, my peace of mind, and my ability to invest in relationships outside of work. Those were the tradeoffs inherent in my choices given my capacity at the time.

Currently, the pendulum of my life has swung in the opposite direction. I've used some of my savings from my early 20's to change my priorities and invest more in my health (physical and mental), my freedom (in terms of ownership over my time), and my relationships (family, romantic, friends, etc). This has undoubtedly been a great investment for me in myself, but has also left me feeling a bit lost and alone at times in search of the deeper meaning and purpose I used to get from solving hard problems as part of a team greater than myself. By optimizing around new priorities, I traded some "best" aspects of my old life for a new set of "best" aspects in my new life. 

No matter how you define "best" in this equation, there will always be some element(s) that get left out. Fundamentally, there is no objective right answer here. Whatever you optimize towards will naturally guide you away from other elements that you care about. Certain things in this equation are orthogonal to one another, and thus by saying yes to one you inherently say no to the other (or at least shades of yes and no, as not all tradeoffs are binary).

At the end of the day, this type of problem has a degree of subjectivity to it. The answer depends on the audience (you) and the context of your current environment and frame of reference to determine what best means to you at this point in time in your life. It's the type of problem that by definition is a moving target that will naturally change as your lived experience changes your perspective and your goals/desires/etc shift as a result. 

Using the optimization mental model here to try to find an objective "best" life doesn't work. It will never be a reliable way to guide you to a clear answer. Whatever answer you find will be fragile and short-lived at best.

We need a new mental model.


## Liminal Space

Ok... so how should I approach these subjective "moving target" questions? What's a better way to think about these types of dynamic fundamental problems?

Today, I'd like to explore thinking about these vague subjective problems using the lens of *liminal space*. 

Liminal space is a term that has multiple definitions depending on the domain. In AI (and mathmatics), liminal space refers to all the unmapped space that exists in a multi-dimentional plane. In architecture, liminal space is the part of a room or building that is empty and a transition zone meant for passing through (like a hallway or an empty backroom). Regardless of the context, liminal space always refers to the idea of "unexplored terrain" - an area of mapped (or unmapped) territory that is not currently occupied, covered, or traversed at the present moment. 

Personally, I believe that our lives contain liminal space as well (not in the physical sense of the word but in the metaphysical sense). By imagining that we all reside on a continuum of human thought and experience, I believe that visualizing where you currently reside on that spectrum and where the liminal space is in your life can help give a better frame of reference for tackling complex problems and better understanding your current situation. While not perfect, this lens provides more freedom than the optimization approach and allows for the ability to live in contradiction to past identities while still appreciating the nuance and complexity of the whole. There doesn't need to be an objective "best" answer. There only needs to be more liminal space to explore.


## Mathmatical Mental Models

To explore this idea further, I want to take a detour to the world of mathmatics to help visualize this concept to make it easier to digest the mental model I want you to use.

As we grow up, all of us are eventually introduced to math classes where we have to graphically understand how mathmatical equations map onto 2D space.

Think of the following equation:

> *y = 2x - 3*

![algebra-equation-a](/images/algebra-equation-a.png)

In this 2D graph, you can see that this function "maps" to various parts of the 2D space. Some coordinates, like `(5, 7)`, live in the "mapped" space (the line the equation represents includes them) while other coordinates, like `(10, 2)`, live in the unmapped space of the graph. Naturally, by having a function that defines which part of the 2D space we care about, we create certain parts of that space that are well represented and certain parts that are left unmapped. I think of the unmapped space of this graphical 2D world as the *liminal space*.

Let's say I decide it is important to me that we map both `(5,7)` and `(10, 2)` instead of leaving either coordinate floating in liminal space. Both coordinates are near and dear to my heart and I care that both are represented by the function we choose. What can we do?

Easy! We can alter our equation that we are using to map the 2D space to ensure both coordinates are represented. Instead of using the original equation (`y = 2x - 3`), let's use the following equation:

> *y = -x + 12*

![algebra-equation-b](/images/algebra-equation-b.png)

Great! We did it! You can see in the graph that both coordinates `(5,7)` and `(10, 2)` are mapped by our new function. By changing the values of the parameters `m` and `b` in our linear equation (`y = mx + b`) we are able to alter the part of the 2D space that is mapped and thus pull a coordinate out of liminal space into the well represented bucket. By choosing a different function, we were able to pick which coordinates we care most about in our 2D world.

But... this came at a cost. 

You may have already noticed the new presence of a third coordinate `(0, -3)` in the diagram. This is a coordinate that was represented by our previous function but is no longer mapped by our new linear equation. By changing our equation, we inherently prioritized the coordinate `(10, 2)` over the coordinate `(0, -3)` and thus pulled `(0, -3)` out of mapped space into liminal space as a result.

Mathmatically, there is no way to represent all three coordinates (`(0, -3)`, `(5, 7)`, and `(10, 2)`) with a linear equation. With only a line at our disposal to map 2D space, these coordinates live orthoginal to each other thus forcing us to prioritize at most two at the expense of the third. No matter what line we choose, at least one coordinate will always be left out. That tradeoff is unavoidable given our constraints. 

But... what if we decided to change our constraint? What if we gave ourselves more parameters to work with to try to map our 2D world and didn't limit ourselves to using just a line?

Great idea! The next step up from a linear equation (`y = mx + b`) is a quatratic equation (`y = ax^2 + bx + c`). Instead of just 2 parameters to manipulate (`m` and `b`), quadratic equations have 3 parameters for us to play around with (`a`, `b`, and `c`). This gives us more options with which to map our 2D plane. Using quadratic equations, we can map all three coordinates we were referencing earlier as we now have enough parameters to expand our mapped 2D space in a more complex way. To show this, let's look at the following quadratic equation:

> *y = -0.3x^2 + 3.5x - 3*

![algebra-equation-c](/images/algebra-equation-c.png)

As you can see from this graph, with a quadratic equation we can properly map all three coordinates we cared about above using one function. The ability to manipulate 3 parameters gives us more room to explore the 2D space in more complex ways. We can create parabolas and can also still create lines (linear equations) if needed by zeroing out the `a` parameter value in the quadratic formula: `y = ax^2 + bx + c`. 

With this increased capacity (3 parameters over 2), the tradeoff that existed before is no longer present. The three coordinates we care about are no longer orthogonal to each other. Increasing our capacity helped us remove the artificial constraint that underpinned the tradeoff we previously had to make (when we were limited in our parameter capacity). In a world where we only had the capacity to create a line, we had tradeoffs that we could not avoid. As our capacity improved to a higher number of parameters (upgrading from linear equations to quadratic equations), we were able to create more complex shapes and map different parts of our 2D space without needing to compromise as heavily as we had to with more limited tools. Increasing capacity led to our ability to handle more nuanced and complex coordinate combinations and reduced the orthogonality of our data set.

That's great! More parameters leads to more control over how we map our 2D world. But even with the increased capacity we still have plenty of coordinates (like `(10, 10)`) that remain unmapped. Just like in our prior example, in the world where we are limited to quadratic equations (3 parameters to manipulate), there is no way to map all four coordinates we have in our diagram (`(10, 10)`, `(0, -3)`, `(5, 7)`, and `(10, 2)`). Given the constraint, these coordinates are orthogonal to each other. We haven't removed our ability to be constrained. We've just leveled up to a higher order complexity and orthogonality.

Ok... I get it. You can't map the whole 2D space. Tradeoffs and orthogonality will always exist depending how disperse our data set is and how many parameters we have to play with to try to map the space. But even so, couldn't we just do the same thing we did before when we upgraded from a linear equation to a quatric equation? If we just continue increasing our capacity (in terms of our number of parameters in our polynomial function) shouldn't we be able to create a complex function that maps to all the coordinates we care about? With infinite parameters (aka: `y = ax^n + bx^(n-1) + cx^(n-2) + .... + z` where n is potentially equal to infinity), we would have infinite flexibility to draw any curve we desire... right?

Very clever. Yes, with infinite parameters to manipulate we can fit our function to any collection of coordinates that is known ahead of time (aka: any curve, shape, or wave within a 2D plane). In mathmatics, the concept of an infinite parameter polynomial is called a [Power series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series). It's a powerful tool but, even with infinite possibilities, the same catch as before still exists. While we can fit any possible curve to any possible collection of coordinate points (assuming two points don't share the same x-coordinate - this is called the ["vertical line test"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=96VqHrvZdXw&t=1&themeRefresh=1)), we cannot fit all possible curves, shapes, or waves that exist in 2D space all at the same time. Even with infinite parameters, certain coordinates will always be orthogonal to one another. Just like we saw above when we were limited with our capacity (2 parameters for linear equations and 3 parameters quadratic equations), no matter what equation we end up with there will always be liminal space that remains unmapped in our 2D world. 

Infinite parameters and infinite capacity cannot solve every orthogonality problem and cannot remove every tradeoff. A function can only map so much of 2D space and by definition will never be able to cover the full space at the same time. By prioritizing one are of the space, other areas of liminal space will be created. It's a moving target, just like our fuzzy subjective problem we encountered above. However you define the coordinates you want to map towards, if the definition of which coordinates you care about are orthogonal to each other you will never be able to find a line of best fit. Assuming you've already maxxed out your capacity (aka: you're working with infinite flexibility to map the 2D space of your life), you're never going to be able to optimize your way to a solution that works long term.

> <u>NOTE</u>: This concept of infinite polynomials is the format that most neural network neurons use ([perceptrons](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QryOsVWbr2c) too) when encoding information. We will get to this in a bit but generally when people talk about updating weights and biases during AI training, they are referring to updating the parameters in the polynomial that represents various neurons in the neural network. In machine learning, these matricies of parameters are often in much higher dimentionality than just 2D, but the mental model we introduced above still holds. MIT has a great (free) course called ["Introduction to Deep Learning"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=alfdI7S6wCY) that goes over this concept in more depth.

## The Novelty Paradox

The older I get the more I realize that most things I've stumbled across have been done before. Most experiences I've encountered have been encountered by someone else that came before me or are being encountered by someone else in a different part of the world (or maybe even right down the street) right now. Most every problem I've worked hard to understand and solve has likely been solved by someone else or has been thought thru at a deeper depth than I've explored it and is understood to a more fundamental degree than my brain can currently grasp. Pretty much every novel idea I come up with has likely been synthesized already with a theory attached to it in jargon I don't yet understand and is published in a book or website that I'm not yet aware exists. From a pure numbers perspective, the odds that one of the 8 billion people on Earth (or one of the 100+ billion people that once lived on Earth) has discovered an idea before me and explored it to more depth than me is pretty high. 

We all percieve our experiences as novel. We think of our thoughts as our own and our lessons as unique and special to our individual human experience. While it is true that we are unique as human beings (our genes are unique and we exist in novel context relative to those who came before us), from a broad strokes perspective our thoughts, ideas, actions, and experiences for the most part are not. We are influenced by those that come before us and are operating in spaces that exist outside our local community (whether we know it or not). If we zoom out to a societal lens (or a greater historical lens across humanity), I'd confidently bet that most of us are living in space (metaphorically) that has already been explored, mapped, and understood to a greater extent than we individually will ever fully comprehend. Most of us are wandering through life rediscovering experiences already known to those that came before us and relearning solved problems without the awareness or knowledge that we are doing so.

This, for the record, is in itself not a bad thing. There is nothing inherently wrong with reliving experiences and relearning lessons individually that have already been explored by others that came before. But if your identity or your "value" (in both a personal and professional setting) is tied to the need to be on the cutting edge of liminal space, awareness of where those before you have gone is vital to knowing what novelty in your life truly means.

## Liminal Space

Ok... let's say I agree with you. Why does it matter if I'm living in "known" space that has already been played out by a different person in a different time and a different environment before me? Do I really need novelty in my human experience for it to be valuable and worth living?

Short answer, no. Of course not. If you enjoy your life and the known (metaphorical) space you live in, it does not matter that you don't break out of the novelty of that that comes before you. But it does mean that you live in some degree of ignorance of what your life could be. If you were to broaden your awareness, you could potentially not have to compromise on things in your life that you currently compromise. Every problem you have someone has solved before you, so if you were to leverage their knowledge in addition to your own you could live an even better life that is less constrained than your current path.

Depends on the goal and what we want out of life. 



There is the individual experience and our awareness within it. And there is the universal totality of the human experience and all we've come across. ## Outline

- What is Liminal Space?
    - The space in between
    - The non-optimized frontier
    - Both metaphorically in life and literally in AI tech
- AI is just Math
    - How to think of and visualize liminal space in terms of AI?
        1. Think of a 2D plane (like from back in algebra)
        2. Create a plot of points in the plane (these are your outputs)
        3. Try to draw a line that fits to all the points you drew (this is what your AI is doing during training and fine-tuning, changing it weights and biases to try to move this line around the 2D space until it finds a fit that consistently gets rewarded by users for the output it provides given any input)

        Lessons:
            y = ax + b (line)
            y = nx^2 + ax + b (parabola)
            y = mx^3 + nx^2 + ax + b (higher-order polinomial function)

            As you add more exponents (x, x^2, x^3, etc...) and move around the coefficients, or parameters (a, b, n, m, etc...) you create new funcitons that map over different types of multi-dimentional spaces. In 2D this is pretty easy to visualize (changing a, b, n, m, etc...) create new functions and you get more "curves" or "bends" as you get higher order polynomials (higher x^n values). With enough exponents and enough data (output points), we can "train" to find the coefficients on a large enough model (the x^n max value) to find the line of best fit for a given space. 

            Latent space is any "whitespace" that is not mapped by the line of best fit (aka: anywhere in the 2D plane that doesn't have the function going through it).

            Living in the latent space means the function can't "map" to where you are (without retraining).

    - Dimensionality of the data (embedding space) vs dimensionality of the parameter count
        - The embedding space dimensionality is how complex the data we're representing is (for example some data points can be a single value, some require multiple values, some require many values). This correlates to the complexity of what you are trying to map. More complex things with take up more dimensions in the embedding space, so once you hit a threshold of dimensionality you can represent problems you couldn't previously.
        - The parameter count is the number of neurons in each later of the neural network. Just like the number of parameters in our math function, more parameters means we can get a more complex graph to map onto the embedding space. The density of the neural network 

    - How to conceptualize multiple neural network layers?
        1. Each layer is a math function (f(x)) working over a given dimensional space (2D, 3D, 4D, etc) with a given parameter count to map over said space (this is the a, b, m, n in the math function concept above)
        2. The output of one function is the input to the next (f(g(x))...). So input data is mapped and warped over the output space with each function.
        3. Less layers means you can "warp" data less between input and output. You have less possible "solutions" to map well over the embedding space.
        4. More data layers mean you can have more attempts to transform data and a better chance to find a group of functions that matches the desired output. There could potentionally be multiple "correct" answers.

    - Higher dimentional liminal space
        Two dimensions is relatively easy to concieve (as most of us took the math classes growing up to understand algebra and can think of points in a 2D plane). This concept is much harder to understand over higher dimensional spaces (3D, 4D, 8D, 1MD, etc...)

        Think of a 3D plot and how much more of it you can "map" over than you could with just a 2D plane. A lower dimensional function is never going to be able to accurately represent a higher dimensional space. 2D can't cover all points on a 3D plane. 3D can't cover all points on a 4D plane. And on and on...
        Naturally, you are always restricted by the dimensionality of your data space, the number of exponents you have in a given function to map that space (your neural net density per layer), and the time (compute) you have to train to find the line of best fit.

        In AI today, we live in many dimensions. Frontier AI labs don't share the dimensionality publically, but estimates put the embedding space it maps to in the tens of thousands of dimensions (if not more). Even open-source AI tools (like Llama) have embeddings in the 4k dimensions range. In addition to the embedding space dimensionality, current AI foundational models (like claude, gemini, GPT, etc) have somewhere in the order of hundreds of neural net layers (post attention transformer) each with hundreds of thousands of parameters. To bring that back to our discussion above that means there are 100+ mathmatical functions mapping over at least 4k+ dimensional data space with 100k possible parameters each. In total this creates roughly hundreds of billions of parameters across the full model. The combo of these values mean any concept that can be represented in 4k+ dimensional space can likely be mapped to an output value that matches well with the 100B+ parameters that are tweaked during training of these neural nets. Crazy...

- Subjective vs Objective Goals
    - Objective goals are ones with a clear "correct" answer regardless of the audience. Some examples include labeling, any game with clear rules, and any task with a verifiable output (works vs. doesn't work).
    - Subjective goals are ones where the grading of "correctness" is relative to the environment and the audience. For example, whether a piece of art or writing is "good" is highly dependent on the audience and the environment that audience is in (what's their emotional state? how variable is that state? does the framing of the writing change how the audience views it? is the audience aware of what I'm trying to do? etc?)
    - Objective goals are solvable over a large enough dimensional space with enough data and neural net layers + parameters.
        - As models get larger, more data becomes available, and objective output goals are better defined, AIs will map over the multi-dimensional input space better and better.
        - Lesson: Don't tie your identity or life to objective tasks, as on the current tradjectory it's likely AI will be able to do it better and cheaper sooner rather than later
    - Subjective goals on the other hand are dependent on the audience and thus are less likely to be automated (especially if the audience itself is a moving target that changes over time). Ex: Human relationships are complex and don't have a "solution" that an AI can map to. Why? Because humans as an audience change their behavior based on the environment, their moods, and what is said to them from other actors in the environment. Someone who maybe loves something one day may hate it the next if there is a change in the environment around it. Behavior and preference change over time. They are subjective. They can be mapped at a snapshot in time but it's very hard to map them continuously as they change.

- Training on specific data creates models that map to the provided data (and thus inherently "funnels" into standard output scenarios)
    1. Data provided is what the model aims to map "towards"
    2. Whatever it maps towards will naturally cause it to standardize around a function that rewards the most common scenarios
    3. Output of the AI model will funnel people into the scenarios that are most rewarded... aka: assuming the data dimensionality is not enough to match reality, there will always be liminal space remaining to play in

    Caveat: If we are mapping a simple space, if that space has objective output goals, and we have enough neural net depth to map the edge cases fully, then it's possible for AI to solve certain data spaces. I'm just skeptical that reality is simple enough to map into a multi-dimensional data plane or objective enough to create a reward function


- Learning to live in the Liminal Space
    - I used to be an engineer with clear objective goals and KPI's (faster, cheaper, etc.)
    - As AI becomes more prevalent, professionally and personally I'm trying to learn to live in the liminal space that is hard to "map" and hard to optimize towards. My value is more than the objective task I can complete and I'm not an efficiency machine. My value lives in the liminal space and I need to find more ways to show that value and make it clear.
    - Living in the liminal space means playing your own game. Most people follow the traditional paths and the routhes that are already mapped out. They try to optimize within a predefined space and learn a skill so they can convince others to hire them because they can get the task done better than other people. That way of thinking leads to valuing the ability to do objective skills, which is the space that is at risk of getting automated away. It's fragile and outdated in my opinion. 
    - As AI wave changes our world, I think it's important to subjectively understand where you stand out and how you can live in an unoccupied space. This I think is going to be the secret to surviving the AI wave from an employment perspective and will be the way to live a more unique and personally fulfilling life outside of work.
